For more than fifteen years I have worked at the intersection of digital design
processes, sustainable construction and life cycle-based environmental
assessment. Digitalisation in construction has mostly been used to raise
productivity and build faster. I think it should also be used to improve
social and ecological quality, because how we produce and use buildings
sits close right at the centre of the climate crisis and how we live through it.


<h2>How I work</h2>

Most of my work sits at the interface between science and policy: exploratory
studies and commissioned research on decarbonising the building sector, much
of it for public authorities and the European Commission. I develop the
metrics, methods and models that capture greenhouse gas emissions and other
environmental impacts across the whole life cycle, and then support the
legislative processes that turn them into reporting duties and target values.

The underlying conviction is that deep decarbonisation is not only a technical
problem. It requires changing a sociotechnical system — the interlinked mix of
technologies, infrastructures, organisations, markets, regulations and
practices that together produce our built environment. That shapes how I pick what to work
on. I think about three ways of scaling, and I try to keep all three alive at
once:

**Scaling up** means supporting change of the rules of the game: establishing the life
cycle perspective in law and policy. This is where the European Commission
work sits, and where the EPBD's new whole life carbon disclosure requirement
and the EU Taxonomy become levers.

**Scaling wide** means reaching greater numbers: building the data, networks
and capacity that let many actors act at once, in many places. Our INDICATE National Building LCA Data and Policy Accelerator and
the Global Building Data Initiative (GBDI) are both attempts to make proven methods
available rather than keep it expert-only, with particular attention to Global
South regions, where most of the new buildings and infrastructure of the coming decades will be
built.

**Scaling deep** means working with the small networks of people pioneering
radical alternatives — bio-based and earth-based construction, low-tech
passive design, community-led housing, sufficiency strategies that reduce how
much we need to build at all. These rarely scale directly, but they are where
conventions get challenged and new norms start growing.

As a rule of thumb for what success looks like: greenhouse gas emissions from
construction should at least halve every decade. Getting there needs
unambiguous benchmarks, obligations anchored in national laws, and
buildings climate and environmental impacts considered across their whole life cycle and supply chains.

<h2>Off work, off the ground</h2>

I live in Vienna with my wife and daughter. When I am not working I spend time with them and in various community-led initiatives working towards a good life for all. I also run, cycle, or climb and frequently take time to enjoy the mountains. I fly paragliders on hike&fly and cross-country trips throughout the Alps. Flying is a great teacher. You cannot make the air do anything. You can only read it, put yourself in the right place, and commit.
Flying rewards patience and punishes wishful thinking, and it teaches you the
difference between them faster than most things. And sometimes it shows you true magic.

<h2>Some lessons from the air</h2>

Much of what the mountains and flying teaches transfers directly to my work of trying to inform better ways of buildings and the collective efforts for systemic transformation at large.

<dl class="lessons">

<dt>Read the landscape</dt>
<dd>Study the terrain to understand where energy is likely to turn into
rising air, and where the valley wind will wash you down instead of lifting
you up. The same discipline applies to a sociotechnical system: before
intervening, learn where effort converts into movement and where it is
absorbed.</dd>

<dt>Anticipate where you will be, and when</dt>
<dd>You plan for conditions that do not exist yet, because by the time you
arrive the day will have moved on. Buildings work the same way. What is
designed today determines emissions for decades, so the assessment has to
model the future state rather than the present one.</dd>

<dt>Use external forces, don't fight them</dt>
<dd>A wing has no engine. Everything you achieve comes from forces already
in the air, used well. Regulation, finance and market momentum are the
equivalent — far more is achieved by aligning with them than by pushing
against them.</dd>

<dt>It is easier together</dt>
<dd>You learn from other pilots, and you read the relative movement of the
gliders around you to optimise your own line. Research works the same way:
consortia, shared data and open methods let everyone see where the lift is.</dd>

<dt>Choose a challenge that fits your passion</dt>
<dd>Not everyone has to do everything. Reviewing your own aspirations and
possibilities honestly, and asking where your passion aligns with impact, is
how you find the task you will actually sustain.</dd>

<dt>Learn from nature</dt>
<dd>Observe what nature is already telling you. Birds circling in an
otherwise invisible thermal mark your next source of lift. Good indicators
make the invisible legible, which is more or less the whole purpose of life
cycle assessment.</dd>

<dt>Stay high</dt>
<dd>Altitude is options. Move slowly through rising air and quickly through
sink — spend time where progress is being made, and cross the rest without
lingering.</dd>

<dt>Recentre to make the most of every lift</dt>
<dd>When you find something that works, adjust until you are getting the
most from it, rather than accepting the first version and moving on.</dd>

<dt>Patience — it is only over when it is over</dt>
<dd>Low saves take what feels like forever. Scratching close to the ground,
highly attentive, reading the landscape, watching nature's indications,
carefully feeling the signals of the wing. And often, somehow, suddenly, a
small lift turns into a persistent one, a thermal breaks through an
inversion, and within moments you may be back at cloudbase.</dd>

</dl>

<h2>Roles and advice</h2>

I advise and support the European Commission, the International Energy Agency
and the United Nations Environment Programme on whole life carbon measurement
and on what can actually reduce emissions in practice. I currently serve as
Lead Advisor on Embodied Carbon for the UNEP GlobalABC Global Status Report
for Buildings and Construction, and contribute to the forthcoming Assessment
Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

I serve on the boards of the radical building sector decarbonisation alliance
[Habitat2030](https://habitat2030.at/) and the collaborative housing pioneers
[Wohnprojekt Wien](https://wohnprojekt.wien/). I am a founding member of the
[Low Carbon Construction Association](https://www.lowcarbon.construction/) and
co-founded [wohnlabor](https://www.wohnlabor.at/), an association for
participatory housing research.

I hold a dual PhD in Architectural Engineering (KU Leuven) and Civil
Engineering Science (TU Graz), completed as a DOC Fellow of the Austrian
Academy of Sciences. Before that I trained and worked as architect and
building systems engineer.
