<div class="page-head wrap narrow">
  <h1>Approach</h1>
  <p class="lede">Deep decarbonisation of the built environment is not merely a
  technical problem. It is a question of changing a system — the interlinked
  mix of technologies, infrastructures, organisations, markets, regulations
  and practices that together produce buildings and infrastructures.</p>
</div>

<main id="content">

<section class="band">
<div class="wrap narrow">

<p>That framing decides what we work on. A method that nobody uses changes
nothing. A regulation without credible data behind it gets watered down in
consultation. An innovation that works beautifully on only one site stays
limited in reach. Each of those is a different kind of challenge, and they
need different kinds of intervention. We think about all three, and try to
bring them all forward at once.</p>

</div>
</section>

<section class="band">
<div class="wrap narrow">

<h2>Scaling up — changing the rules</h2>

<p>The science-to-policy interface: establishing the life cycle perspective in
law and making it operational.</p>

<p>Our work here has moved from single-building assessment to transnational
building stock modelling. We led the analysis behind the European Commission's
roadmap for reducing whole life carbon of buildings, and quantified the
baseline and future scenarios for emissions and removals across the EU building
stock to 2050. That evidence feeds instruments with teeth — the revised EPBD,
which will require whole life carbon disclosure for all new buildings by 2030,
and the EU Taxonomy, which steers where capital goes.</p>

<p>The next step is widening the lens beyond greenhouse gases: a full
environmental footprint, and the social and economic dimensions of a transition
that has to be just as well as fast.</p>

<h2>Scaling wide — reaching greater numbers</h2>

<p>Methods only matter at the scale they are used. Much of our work is
therefore building the shared infrastructure that lets many actors act at
once.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.indicatedata.com/">INDICATE</a> is a funding and
support framework helping national consortia generate the building LCA data
their governments need to set benchmarks — piloted in Ireland, Spain and
Czechia, now running in Austria, Hungary, Italy and Luxembourg under the EU
LIFE programme. <a href="https://www.gbdi.io">GBDI</a>, developed with EPFL
under the GLOBE consensus, is building an open global data commons on resource
use and embodied carbon, with particular attention to the Global South, where
most of the floor area of the coming decades will be built.</p>

<p>Both began as pilots and both outgrew them. Both now have lives of their
own, which is the point.</p>

<h2>Scaling deep — working with pioneers</h2>

<p>The third strand is the smallest in budget and the least comfortable in a
policy meeting, and we think it is where the interesting questions live.</p>

<p>Here we work with the networks pioneering radical alternatives: bio-based
and earth-based construction, low-tech passive design, community-led and
collaborative housing, reuse of existing structures, and sufficiency strategies
that reduce how much needs to be built in the first place. These rarely scale by
replication. They matter because they test what is possible, and because they
shift what the mainstream considers normal.</p>

<p>This is also where we guard against carbon tunnel vision. An intervention
that cuts emissions while worsening affordability, biodiversity or material
justice is not a success, it is a transfer.</p>

<h2>Growth, and what follows it</h2>

<p>Most decarbonisation strategy for construction assumes the sector keeps
growing and gets cleaner per unit built. That assumption is doing a lot of
work, and the arithmetic is uncomfortable: global floor area is projected to
rise by up to 150% by 2050, which is roughly five billion square metres of new
building every year. Efficiency gains that would be impressive against a stable
stock look much less so against that.</p>

<p>We take the post-growth question seriously — not as a slogan, but as a
research agenda. What would it mean to meet housing and infrastructure needs
with radically less new material? Which existing buildings should never be
replaced? What does sufficiency look like as a measurable strategy rather than
a virtue?</p>

<p>We are not neutral about this, and we are also not naive about it. The
instruments that exist today — benchmarks, disclosure duties, taxonomies — were
built for a growth paradigm, and they are still the best available lever for
cutting emissions this decade. So we help build them, well, while doing the
research that asks what comes after.</p>

<p>Holding that tension is not a compromise between two positions. It is the
position.</p>

<section class="band">
<div class="wrap narrow">

<h2>Methods</h2>

<p>Our work combines life cycle sustainability assessment with building data
science. In practice that means bottom-up building stock models,
archetype-based characterisation, statistical analysis of environmental product
declarations and building LCA case data, scenario modelling to 2050, and
open-source tooling in Python.</p>

<p>We publish our methods, data and code openly wherever licensing allows. A
benchmark nobody can reproduce is an opinion.</p>
