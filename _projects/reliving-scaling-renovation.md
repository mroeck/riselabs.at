<h2>Context</h2>

Renovation is where the largest opportunity in the built environment sits, and
it is the one that consistently fails to scale. It stays fragmented, gets
reduced to a technical upgrade, and is argued for one building at a time.

Re:Living, part of the VELUX Living Places programme, treats renovation
instead as a systemic transformation - one that has to link environmental
impact, health and affordability at once, because in Europe's housing those
three are the same problem seen from different sides.

RISE's contribution is the part that turns the argument into something
rankable: a hierarchy for action, and an estimate of what following it would
actually be worth.

<h2>The argument</h2>

<div class="findings" markdown="1">

- **The buildings are already here.** Around 85% of today's European building
  stock is expected to still be in use in 2050. Any strategy that treats new
  construction as the main instrument is working on the small end of the
  problem.
- **The crises are one crisis.** Climate and biodiversity, energy and
  resources, housing affordability, and unhealthy homes are usually addressed
  by separate policy. Over 40 million Europeans are in energy poverty, a third
  are exposed to indoor climate hazards, and house prices rose 53% between
  2015 and 2024 - the same stock, failing on four counts.
- **The space problem is a distribution problem.** 20% of dwellings are
  underoccupied while 16.8% are overcrowded, and the EU needs more than two
  million additional homes a year - about a third more than it builds. Some of
  that demand can be met by using what exists differently rather than by
  building more of it.

</div>

<h2>Objectives</h2>

The paper's central device, in strict order of preference:

<div class="findings" markdown="1">

- **Renovate - first.** Reclaim, reshape, refine, repurpose, resize. These
  unlock capacity and cut energy use for the least material. Reshaping an
  underused dwelling uses roughly five times less material than compact new
  build.
- **Extend - where renovation cannot meet demand.** Attic conversion, building
  up, building out.
- **Build new - last resort.** Compact construction, with biobased preferred
  over conventional mineral construction, which the paper treats as the
  reference case to move away from rather than the default.

</div>

Alongside it sits a four-quadrant reading of material intensity against
emissions impact, which separates what looks like progress from what is: a
measure can cut emissions and still sit in the wrong quadrant if it does so by
pouring in material.

<h2>What following it would be worth</h2>

<div class="findings" markdown="1">

- **Capacity.** Using buildings better could unlock 50–107 million dwellings
  and 4–9 billion m² of floor area - housing for roughly 120–250 million
  people, without new land.
- **Emissions.** 3.9–19.1 billion tonnes CO₂e avoided over fifty years, which
  is 8–32% of annual building-sector whole life carbon and 2–11% of EU-wide
  annual emissions. About three quarters of that comes from renovation
  measures, and the renovation measures pay their carbon back in three to
  seven years.
- **Material.** 5.8–13 Gt avoided, roughly two years of total EU material
  consumption.
- **Cost, where it has been tested.** Some completed conversion projects
  delivered homes up to 20% cheaper than new construction.

</div>

<h2>What this is not</h2>

The quantification is a theoretical and technical potential, and the paper
says so plainly. It does not model demographic change, local market dynamics
or future grid decarbonisation, and it does not pretend the economic, social
and regulatory barriers are small. The number is what is physically available
if the hierarchy were followed - the argument is about how much is being left
on the table, not about what will happen by itself.
